# 6FOLIO Asset Guidelines

**Document type:** Visual asset, media, and production standards  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** First-release asset specification  
**Brand idea:** Six perspectives. One clear direction.

---

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how imagery, video, illustrations, icons, mockups, and other visual material should be selected, created, prepared, stored, and used across the 6FOLIO website.

The purpose is not to fill empty spaces with attractive images. Every asset should help explain the agency, show the quality of its thinking, make a project easier to understand, or give the website a recognisable visual character.

The visual material should feel art-directed and connected. It should not look like a random collection of stock images, generic AI illustrations, unrelated mockups, and decorative graphics.

Read this document alongside:

- `AGENTS.md` for brand integrity, workflow, and approval boundaries.
- `6folio_agency_blueprint.md` for agency positioning and service pillars.
- `6folio_website_story_and_copy.md` for the six-person story, page content, and concept-project directions.
- `DESIGN_SYSTEM.md` for colour, typography, composition, and interface rules.
- `TECH_STACK.md` for implementation and image-delivery conventions.

If the repository contains approved assets or a brand identity, preserve them. Do not replace supplied photography, logos, or project material with generated alternatives without a clear reason and approval.

## 2. The 6FOLIO visual point of view

6FOLIO is built around six collaborators with different but complementary perspectives. The visual system should express collaboration, observation, craft, and connected thinking.

The six-person story is a narrative foundation, not a requirement to show six people in every image or repeat the numeral six as a graphic motif. The team can be represented through real working moments, individual points of view, shared production, and the movement from an initial brief to a finished outcome.

### 2.1 Desired visual qualities

- Human and considered.
- Editorial rather than corporate stock photography.
- Contemporary without relying on a short-lived visual trend.
- Tactile, with believable materials, lighting, and details.
- Specific to the subject being shown.
- Consistent in colour treatment, crop, and overall quality.
- Honest about what is real, self-initiated, illustrative, or conceptual.

### 2.2 The visual test

Before adding an asset, ask:

1. What does this asset communicate?
2. Does it connect to the page, service, story, or project?
3. Would the page become less clear or less memorable without it?
4. Does it fit the image treatment and quality of the rest of the site?
5. Do we have the right to use it in this context?
6. Could a visitor reasonably mistake it for a real client, team member, campaign, or result?

If the asset has no clear purpose, leave it out.

## 3. Asset priority and authenticity

Use assets in this order of preference:

1. Approved original material created by the 6FOLIO team.
2. Original project artwork, photography, video, or interface designs made specifically for a clearly labelled concept project.
3. Properly licensed third-party material that fits the brief and has recorded usage rights.
4. Carefully created diagrams, typographic compositions, or abstract visual systems where photography would add no real value.
5. Generated imagery only when it is genuinely appropriate, transparently treated as illustrative, and does not pretend to document a real person, shoot, client, product, or event.

Do not use generic generated imagery as a shortcut for real production capability. The agency includes photography and videography disciplines. As soon as practical, create a small original image library that demonstrates the team's own eye and production standards.

### 3.1 Never fabricate evidence

Do not create or imply any of the following:

- A real client photoshoot that did not happen.
- A real campaign, event, office, product, or team gathering that did not happen.
- A named person or employee who is not part of the team.
- A client logo, endorsement, testimonial, award, press mention, or partnership that has not been verified.
- Analytics dashboards, revenue figures, audience numbers, or performance improvements presented as measured results when they are fictional.
- A brand identity that implies a fictional concept brand is a real paying client.

Concept projects are allowed and encouraged, but they must be labelled clearly on the portfolio card, case-study page, and any relevant metadata or structured content. Use labels such as **Concept project** or **Self-initiated project**. Their outcomes should be described as intended goals, design decisions, or creative hypotheses, not measured achievements.

### 3.2 Team imagery

Until the six collaborators provide approved photos and names:

- Use role titles, not invented names.
- Do not generate realistic portraits and present them as the founders or employees.
- Do not use unrelated people from stock libraries as if they are the 6FOLIO team.
- Use close-up details of hands at work, shared materials, work surfaces, camera equipment, notebooks, lighting setups, or abstract editorial compositions where a human presence is useful but identity is not required.
- If a team image is needed, use a clearly illustrative composition rather than a deceptive fake group photograph.

When real team portraits become available, use consistent lighting, framing, background treatment, and image quality. The portraits should feel like one considered series, not six unrelated headshots.

## 4. Asset categories and art direction

### 4.1 Photography

Photography should feel purposeful and editorial. Prefer images with a clear subject, believable light, useful negative space, and a considered relationship between foreground and background.

Useful photographic subjects include:

- Original campaign and product photography from self-initiated projects.
- Real process details: lighting setups, contact sheets, camera equipment, set preparation, physical prototypes, paper drafts, and editing workstations.
- Approved team portraits and collaborative working moments.
- Real environments connected to a specific project or service.
- Carefully selected product, architecture, food, or lifestyle images when they are relevant to a concept brief.

Avoid:

- Generic staged business meetings with everyone smiling at a laptop.
- Handshake photographs, floating phones, random office scenes, and unrelated desk flat-lays.
- Overly polished stock images that have no relationship to the project.
- Inconsistent image temperature or saturation within a single sequence.
- Images where the subject is unclear or the crop removes important context.
- Repeated use of the same stock photograph across multiple sections.

**Composition:** Use a mix of wide environmental frames, close details, human-scale moments, and product-focused compositions. Vary image ratios with intent. Do not force every image into the same rectangular card if the layout would be stronger with a more editorial sequence.

**Lighting and treatment:** Prefer natural or carefully controlled light, believable shadows, accurate material rendering, and restrained colour grading. Do not add a heavy filter to make unrelated images appear consistent. Consistency should begin with selection and art direction.

**Cropping:** Keep the important subject inside safe crop areas. Check the desktop, tablet, and mobile crops separately. Use explicit focal points where the image component supports them. Do not hide faces, product details, or meaningful text under overlays.

### 4.2 Video and motion footage

Video should show movement that adds meaning: a product being used, a camera move that reveals a space, a close-up of a production detail, a short campaign sequence, or a carefully edited brand film.

For website use:

- Prefer short, purposeful clips over long background footage.
- Provide a meaningful poster image.
- Keep the first frame visually strong and understandable without sound.
- Never autoplay audio.
- Use muted playback for any autoplaying decorative footage.
- Give users controls for video with meaningful spoken or narrative content.
- Provide captions or subtitles for speech and important audio information.
- Avoid autoplay where it could distract from reading or reduce accessibility.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences. Replace motion-heavy sequences with a static poster or a simpler transition.
- Avoid flashing, rapid cuts, or movement that could cause discomfort.
- Ensure video does not block access to navigation or content.
- Do not use video as a substitute for an accessible explanation of important information.

Use a lightweight delivery strategy. Do not load several large videos immediately on the first screen. Lazy-load non-critical videos and avoid unnecessary third-party embeds. Choose encoding and compression based on visual quality and realistic device bandwidth.

### 4.3 Motion graphics and animation assets

Motion graphics should communicate a sequence, reveal a relationship, or support a distinct creative idea. They should not exist only to make the site appear technically advanced.

Appropriate uses include:

- A short identity reveal, if the logo system supports it.
- A restrained sequence showing how six disciplines connect.
- A case-study transition that explains a process.
- A focused product or campaign motion piece.
- Subtle interface feedback for navigation or state changes.

Avoid:

- Random floating shapes.
- Continuous movement with no informational purpose.
- Large animated backgrounds behind long text.
- Scroll effects that take control away from the visitor.
- Recreating a reference studio's signature animation.

Use CSS for simple transitions. Use GSAP or another motion library only for a clearly justified sequence that needs timeline control. Keep animation code isolated, clean up listeners and timelines, and ensure the page remains useful when the animation does not run.

### 4.4 Illustrations and 3D assets

Illustration and 3D should be used selectively. The primary visual identity should come from typography, photography, graphic composition, and project-specific art direction.

When an illustration is needed:

- Establish one visual style for the set.
- Use a deliberate line weight, shape language, palette, and level of detail.
- Make the illustration specific to the topic rather than using generic abstract blobs.
- Avoid mixing unrelated icon and illustration styles.
- Provide meaningful alternative text when the image communicates information. Use empty alternative text for purely decorative imagery.

When 3D is used:

- It must support a real concept, product, or visual narrative.
- Keep geometry, lighting, textures, and camera movement restrained.
- Avoid adding 3D solely because it is fashionable.
- Consider performance and mobile rendering before using real-time 3D.
- Provide a static fallback for devices or users where the effect is unavailable or reduced.

Do not introduce a WebGL or 3D dependency for a decorative object that can be expressed more clearly as a static image or CSS element.

### 4.5 Logos, marks, and brand graphics

- Use only the approved 6FOLIO logo or wordmark supplied by the team.
- Do not invent a final logo from typed text if an approved logo is expected.
- Do not distort, stretch, rotate, outline, or recolour the logo outside approved variants.
- Maintain sufficient clear space and contrast.
- Do not use client logos without permission or a valid basis for display.
- For concept brands, create original marks and label the project as conceptual.
- Store logo artwork as SVG where appropriate, with a raster fallback where required.

If the final 6FOLIO logo has not been supplied, use a typographic wordmark treatment based on the design system and mark it as provisional in the project notes. Do not let an improvised mark become an unapproved permanent identity.

### 4.6 Icons and interface graphics

Use one coherent icon family or a small set of custom SVG icons. Prefer simple shapes that remain readable at their rendered size.

- Do not mix filled, outlined, emoji, and hand-drawn icon styles in the same navigation or component group.
- Use icons only when they improve recognition or comprehension.
- Pair unfamiliar icons with visible text labels.
- Provide accessible names for icon-only controls.
- Avoid decorative icon rows that repeat information already stated in text.
- Do not use emoji as a replacement for the visual identity.

### 4.7 Device mockups and interface imagery

Mockups may be used to explain a real or concept digital product, but they must not become the main visual language of the whole agency website.

- Show the actual interface inside the device frame.
- Do not use unreadable pseudo-UI or fake dashboard data as proof of a real product.
- Keep screen perspective, device scale, shadows, and lighting believable.
- Prefer a direct high-quality interface capture when a mockup adds no value.
- Make sure text and key interface details remain legible.
- Use device mockups selectively. Avoid a repeated parade of identical laptop and phone frames.
- Mark speculative products and sample interfaces as concept work.

## 5. Asset requirements for the first website

The first release should be complete without requiring a large production shoot. Use a small, coherent set of assets and prioritise quality over quantity.

### 5.1 Minimum useful asset set

| Area | Recommended initial material | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Home hero | One strong original or properly licensed visual, or a typographic composition | Must reinforce the central story and leave room for the headline. |
| Selected work | A coherent cover image or designed visual for each concept project | Each project must be visibly labelled as concept work. |
| Case studies | Several supporting images per project, such as campaign artwork, product details, layouts, or process frames | Use only material that supports the case narrative. |
| Services | A consistent image or graphic treatment for each service, where useful | Do not force six interchangeable stock photos into six cards. |
| About | Real approved team imagery when available; otherwise process details or a designed narrative composition | Never fabricate portraits or team activity. |
| Contact | A calm supporting graphic or no image | Prioritise a clear enquiry path. |
| Social sharing | A custom Open Graph image and a suitable fallback | Use correct brand text and contrast. |
| Site icon | Approved logo mark or an approved simplified monogram | Do not improvise an unapproved permanent logo. |

The website may launch with fewer images if the page composition is strong. It must not launch with random imagery chosen only to fill empty boxes.

### 5.2 Concept project asset direction

The website story and copy document proposes three initial concept directions. Treat them as self-initiated portfolio exercises unless the team later confirms a real client relationship.

For each concept:

1. Define the fictional or exploratory brief.
2. Establish its audience, visual world, and tone.
3. Create a small but coherent art direction.
4. Produce the main visual and supporting applications needed for the case study.
5. Explain what is a design proposal, prototype, or illustrative asset.
6. Mark the project clearly as **Concept project** or **Self-initiated project**.
7. Do not invent real usage, sales, reach, conversion, or campaign results.

A concept case study can demonstrate thinking and craft without pretending that the work was commissioned or tested in the market.

## 6. Image specifications and delivery

### 6.1 Preferred formats

| Format | Use |
|---|---|
| AVIF | Preferred for supported photographic and raster images when visual quality and browser support are verified. |
| WebP | Strong general-purpose web format for photographs and many graphics. |
| JPEG | Fallback or source format for photographs where appropriate. |
| PNG | Use when transparency or lossless raster detail is required. Avoid large photographic PNGs. |
| SVG | Logos, icons, and scalable vector graphics from trusted sources. Sanitize or validate user-supplied SVGs before use. |
| MP4 / WebM | Short web video, with formats selected according to browser support and delivery needs. |

Use responsive image delivery and appropriately sized variants. Do not send a full-resolution original to a small mobile card.

### 6.2 Dimensions and aspect ratios

Choose image dimensions based on the actual component and source material. These are practical starting points, not mandatory crops.

| Placement | Suggested ratio | Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Wide hero visual | 16:9 or wider | Keep the focal subject away from essential text. |
| Editorial project cover | 4:5, 3:4, 1:1, or 16:9 | Choose the ratio that best expresses the project, not one fixed ratio for all cards. |
| Full-width case-study image | 16:9 or original composition | Preserve the art direction and important context. |
| Portrait or team image | 4:5 or 3:4 | Keep framing consistent across a set. |
| Product detail | 1:1, 4:5, or a deliberate crop | Preserve accurate product proportions. |
| Small thumbnail | Based on the component | Ensure the subject remains recognisable at small sizes. |

For every important image, inspect the real crop at common viewport widths. Use `object-fit` and `object-position` intentionally. Do not stretch images to satisfy a layout.

### 6.3 Compression and loading

- Compress images to an appropriate quality level and inspect the result visually.
- Use responsive `srcset` and `sizes`, or the framework's image tooling.
- Set intrinsic width and height or a stable aspect ratio to prevent layout shift.
- Lazy-load below-the-fold imagery.
- Do not lazy-load the primary above-the-fold hero image if doing so harms the initial visual experience or largest-contentful-paint performance.
- Use `fetchpriority="high"` only for the actual critical image, not every image.
- Avoid loading huge image galleries before a visitor needs them.
- Use a consistent placeholder or background where image loading would otherwise cause a harsh flash.
- Test on a slower mobile connection as well as desktop broadband.

### 6.4 Image focal points

For every art-directed image, identify the focal point and confirm it survives responsive crops. Store focal point information in the project content data if the site supports per-image positioning.

Do not let text overlays cover a face, product feature, or other essential subject. If the composition does not support text over the image, place the headline outside it.

## 7. Accessibility and meaning

Images are part of the content experience. Handle them according to their purpose.

### 7.1 Alternative text

- Informative image: write concise alt text describing the information it contributes.
- Functional image: describe the action or destination if the image is part of a control.
- Decorative image: use empty alt text (`alt=""`) so assistive technology can ignore it.
- Complex graphic: provide a nearby text explanation or a structured description. Do not try to put the entire meaning into a very long alt attribute.
- Text inside an image: repeat essential wording in accessible HTML text.

Do not begin every alt description with "image of" or "picture of" unless the medium itself matters.

Do not use filenames, keyword lists, or marketing slogans as alt text.

### 7.2 Video accessibility

- Provide captions for meaningful speech.
- Provide transcripts when video contains important information that cannot be understood from visuals alone.
- Ensure controls are keyboard accessible and have labels.
- Avoid flashing or strobing effects.
- Do not convey essential information only through fast-moving visuals.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences and allow users to pause or stop moving content when required.

### 7.3 Contrast and overlays

Text on images is often difficult to read. Check actual contrast across the whole text area, not only at one sample pixel. Use an intentional solid surface or a restrained overlay when needed, but do not darken every image automatically. Prefer moving the text to a separate layout area when the image is too complex.

## 8. Asset sourcing and usage rights

Every third-party asset must have a known source and permitted use. A file being publicly accessible does not mean it is free to use.

Before using third-party material:

1. Identify the original source, not just the website where it was discovered.
2. Read the licence or terms relevant to the exact asset.
3. Confirm that the intended commercial website use is permitted.
4. Check attribution requirements, modification rights, model releases, and any restrictions on redistribution.
5. Record the source URL, creator, licence, access date, and any attribution text required.
6. Keep a copy of the licence or a reliable record where practical.
7. Do not remove watermarks or use assets that are clearly unlicensed.

Do not scrape images from other agency websites, award galleries, Pinterest, Google Images, Instagram, or social platforms for production use. Those sources may be useful for visual research, but they do not grant reuse rights.

Do not reuse a reference website's exact photographs, campaign artwork, illustrations, layout screenshots, or videos in the 6FOLIO implementation. Learn from principles such as composition, pacing, and hierarchy, then create a distinct expression.

### 8.1 Stock assets

If stock imagery is necessary:

- Use a reputable source and verify the current asset-specific licence.
- Prefer a smaller number of carefully art-directed images over a large set of generic photos.
- Avoid images that look like common template placeholders.
- Check whether a model or property release is required for the intended use.
- Do not imply that stock subjects are 6FOLIO staff, clients, or customers.
- Keep stock credits and licence information in the asset register.

### 8.2 AI-generated assets

Generated assets may be useful for concept exploration, moodboards, fictional campaign directions, or clearly illustrative visual material. They require careful review.

- Never present generated people as real members of the team, clients, or campaign participants.
- Never present generated scenes as documentary evidence of a shoot, office, product, event, or customer interaction.
- Never invent brand marks, product packaging, interface screenshots, or written text that may be mistaken for approved client work.
- Check generated images for malformed details, inconsistent anatomy, incorrect product features, fake logos, and garbled text.
- Do not rely on generated text inside an image. Set important wording in HTML or a proper design file.
- Do not imitate a living artist's distinctive style or reproduce another agency's signature visual identity.
- Record when an asset is generated and what role it plays in the project.
- Confirm that any tool or service used permits the intended commercial use under its current terms.

AI generation is not a substitute for original photography when the goal is to demonstrate the team's real photography or film production capability.

## 9. Asset storage and naming

Keep assets organised, traceable, and easy to replace. Do not leave unexplained files scattered through the project root.

A suggested structure is:

```text
public/
  assets/
    brand/
      logo/
      social/
    home/
    team/
    services/
    work/
      serein-objects/
        cover/
        photography/
        campaign/
        mockups/
      goodkind-coffee/
        cover/
        photography/
        campaign/
        mockups/
      northstar-work/
        cover/
        photography/
        campaign/
        mockups/
    video/
      posters/
      clips/
    icons/
```

Adapt this structure to the existing repository if it already has a clear asset convention.

### 9.1 File naming

Use descriptive, lowercase, hyphen-separated names.

Good examples:

```text
home-hero-studio-process-wide.avif
serein-objects-project-cover-portrait.webp
goodkind-coffee-product-detail-02.avif
northstar-work-campaign-social-01.webp
6folio-og-image.png
```

Avoid:

```text
IMG_8392-final-final.jpg
image1.png
new-banner-latest2.webp
Screenshot 2026-10-31 at 10.30.00 PM.png
```

Do not use special characters, random suffixes, or ambiguous names. Keep original source files in a clearly separated working folder if the team needs to preserve them. Do not serve huge editing originals to the website.

### 9.2 Asset register

Maintain an asset register in the repository, for example `docs/ASSET_REGISTER.md` or a structured data file. For each important external or generated asset, record:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Filename | Exact path and filename |
| Description | What the asset depicts or communicates |
| Type | Original, stock, licensed, generated, icon, logo, or other |
| Source | Creator, source URL, or internal production owner |
| Licence | Exact licence or permission basis |
| Attribution | Required credit, if any |
| Date | Date sourced or created |
| Usage | Pages or components where it appears |
| Status | Approved, needs review, or do not publish |

Do not claim that a licence is verified if it has not been checked. Mark unresolved assets as **Needs review** and do not publish them until the rights issue is resolved.

## 10. Project-specific image systems

The portfolio should not look like one generic template applied to three unrelated imaginary companies. Each concept project needs its own coherent art direction while still sitting comfortably inside the 6FOLIO website.

### 10.1 Shared portfolio standard

All project presentations should share:

- Consistent case-study navigation and information hierarchy.
- A clear project title and concise summary.
- A visible concept-project label where applicable.
- A consistent way to show services and deliverables.
- High-quality imagery with clear focal points.
- A considered sequence from brief to idea to execution.

### 10.2 Distinct project worlds

For each project, choose a distinct visual direction based on the project's audience, product, and brief. The project-specific palette and art direction may differ from the 6FOLIO website. Do not force every project into the agency's warm paper and accent palette.

The case-study page should make it clear which visuals are:

- Proposed identity or campaign artwork.
- Example social or advertising applications.
- Product photography or illustrative concept visuals.
- Website or interface prototypes.
- Process sketches or research artefacts.

Only describe research, testing, or client feedback if it actually happened. Do not create fake workshop photos, participant quotations, or research findings to make a concept project appear more mature.

### 10.3 Project visual sequence

A well-paced case study may use this sequence:

1. Project cover that establishes its visual world.
2. Brief and audience context.
3. The key observation or opportunity.
4. The central creative or strategic idea.
5. Identity, content, or campaign system.
6. Photography, film, or motion applications.
7. Digital experience or marketing touchpoints.
8. A concise explanation of the intended outcome and what would be measured in a real engagement.

Use only the sections that suit the project. Do not make every case study artificially long.

## 11. Website media behaviour

Assets should support a fast, predictable, and accessible experience.

- Prevent layout shift by reserving image and video space before media loads.
- Use loading states only where they help the visitor understand what is happening.
- Avoid blank image frames, broken links, and indefinite skeleton loaders.
- Provide a graceful fallback when a video, animation, or external embed fails.
- Do not make a page dependent on a third-party asset host when a local optimised asset is available and permitted.
- Keep image and video requests to a sensible minimum, particularly on the first viewport.
- Ensure the page still communicates its meaning if non-essential media is blocked.
- Test browser back/forward navigation when pages contain transitions or route-level media.
- Check that sticky content, captions, and overlays do not obscure the subject or controls.

### 11.1 Motion and reduced motion

When `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` is active:

- Remove or simplify non-essential parallax and large movement.
- Stop decorative looping animations where practical.
- Replace scroll-triggered reveals with immediate content visibility.
- Keep state changes and navigation understandable.
- Do not leave content hidden because an animation did not run.

A static fallback must communicate the same essential information as the animated version.

## 12. Visual review checklist

Before an asset or image sequence is approved, review it at desktop and mobile sizes.

### Relevance and integrity

- [ ] The asset has a clear purpose.
- [ ] It is relevant to the page or project.
- [ ] It is original, licensed, or otherwise approved for use.
- [ ] It does not misrepresent a person, client, campaign, location, or result.
- [ ] Concept work is labelled clearly.

### Art direction

- [ ] Lighting, crop, colour treatment, and image quality feel considered.
- [ ] The focal point is clear.
- [ ] The image supports the typography and layout.
- [ ] The image does not feel like a random stock placeholder.
- [ ] Image treatment is consistent within the relevant sequence.
- [ ] The visual has not copied another agency's distinctive expression.

### Technical quality

- [ ] The correct format and dimensions are used.
- [ ] The image is compressed appropriately.
- [ ] Responsive crops have been checked.
- [ ] Width, height, or aspect ratio prevents layout shift.
- [ ] Loading priority is appropriate.
- [ ] Alt text is correct for the image's purpose.
- [ ] Video has a poster and appropriate captions or controls.
- [ ] Reduced-motion behaviour is supported.

### Rights and documentation

- [ ] Source and creator are recorded where relevant.
- [ ] Licence or permission has been checked.
- [ ] Attribution requirements are met.
- [ ] The asset register is updated.
- [ ] Any uncertainty is clearly flagged before publishing.

## 13. Coding-agent implementation instructions

The AI coding agent should follow this sequence:

### Phase A: Inventory

1. Inspect all existing assets and identify their likely purpose.
2. Review the brand files and any supplied photography, video, logos, fonts, and project material.
3. Do not delete or overwrite original assets during the audit.
4. Identify missing hero, project, team, and social-sharing assets.
5. Create an asset inventory and note unknown or unverified licences.

### Phase B: Art direction

1. Read the story and copy document to understand the narrative.
2. Read `DESIGN_SYSTEM.md` for the approved visual foundation.
3. Define a small, consistent asset direction for the home page and each concept project.
4. Use available approved material before sourcing or generating anything new.
5. If an asset is unavailable, choose a composition that can work without it rather than inserting unrelated imagery.
6. Clearly label concept assets and avoid any fictional evidence.

### Phase C: Preparation

1. Organise files using the existing repository convention or the suggested structure in this document.
2. Use clear filenames.
3. Create appropriately sized and compressed image variants.
4. Preserve the original source when needed, outside the public delivery path.
5. Add meaningful alternative text or mark decorative imagery appropriately.
6. Update the asset register with provenance and rights information.

### Phase D: Implementation

1. Use the framework's image delivery strategy described in `TECH_STACK.md`.
2. Add stable dimensions or aspect ratios.
3. Set responsive image sizes and focal points.
4. Lazy-load non-critical media.
5. Avoid loading unnecessary media in the initial viewport.
6. Add poster frames, captions, controls, and reduced-motion fallbacks for video and animation.
7. Test all important crops at desktop, tablet, and mobile widths.

### Phase E: Review

1. Check the rendered page rather than trusting the source files alone.
2. Look for distorted images, incorrect crops, placeholder assets, missing alt text, and licence uncertainty.
3. Check image loading and layout stability.
4. Check the site with reduced motion enabled.
5. Verify that concept work is never mistaken for real client work.
6. Document assets that still need human review or replacement.

## 14. Human approval boundaries

The coding agent may organise, resize, compress, and implement assets that are already approved for use. It may create clearly labelled original concept artwork within the approved brief.

The agent must not:

- Publish an unverified logo as the final 6FOLIO identity.
- Use a team member's likeness without permission.
- Publish third-party assets when the licence is unknown.
- use client logos, client work, or confidential material without approval.
- Invent credentials, awards, testimonials, performance evidence, or partnerships.
- Purchase stock assets, subscribe to asset services, or incur costs without approval.
- Upload private source material to external services without permission.
- Publish, deploy, or connect the website to production services without the required owner approval.

If a required asset is missing, keep the layout complete using typography, CSS, a neutral placeholder treatment, or an explicitly labelled concept visual. Do not fabricate proof. Record the missing asset in the final report.

## 15. Definition of done

The asset work is complete for the first release when:

- [ ] Every important image has a defined role in the content.
- [ ] The home page has a deliberate visual opening.
- [ ] Each portfolio concept has a coherent and distinct visual direction.
- [ ] Concept work is labelled accurately.
- [ ] No fake client, team, campaign, testimonial, or performance evidence is present.
- [ ] All required usage rights are known or unresolved assets are excluded from publishing.
- [ ] Images are correctly sized, compressed, and responsive.
- [ ] Video and motion content have accessible fallbacks.
- [ ] Alternative text is accurate and useful.
- [ ] Asset names and locations are organised.
- [ ] The asset register has been created or updated.
- [ ] The visual system remains consistent with `DESIGN_SYSTEM.md`.
- [ ] Outstanding asset needs are documented for the team.

## 16. Final asset report

At the end of implementation, provide a short report containing:

1. Assets reused from the repository.
2. New assets created for 6FOLIO or its concept projects.
3. Third-party assets used, with their source and recorded licence basis.
4. Assets excluded because rights or authenticity could not be verified.
5. Images or videos that still need original production by the team.
